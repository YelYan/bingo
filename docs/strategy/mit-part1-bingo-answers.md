## The big idea of the whole PDF, in one breath

A business does only two things: **create value** (make something people want) and **capture some of that value** (get paid, so you can do it again). Everything else in the 45 slides is a way to check you are doing those two things well.

Think of a lemonade stand. Value = cold lemonade on a hot day. Capture = 500 kyats a cup. If the cup costs you 600 to make, you created value but captured nothing, and the stand dies.

## Slides 3–7: "Why are you here?" and "So many questions"

### Why are you here? (slide 3) — pick honestly
The slide gives four reasons:
1. I know nothing and want to learn.
2. Something "sucks" and I want to fix it.
3. I have an idea and want to change the world.
4. Entrepreneurs are glamorous and make money.

**Bingo:** Reason 2 is the real one. Myanmar sellers run shops through chat messages, lose track of orders, and parcels vanish with no tracking. That "sucks", and we fix it. Reason 4 (glamour) is the weakest reason; the PDF itself warns about it on slide 9.

## Slide 8: What's the reward? Fame? Fortune?

**Simple answer:** Both are side effects, not goals. The reward worth planning for: sellers whose shops run better because of Bingo, and a company that earns enough to keep improving. Fame and fortune only come if the first part is real.

## Slide 9: The reality — "He built a trillion-dollar company. He wouldn't do it again."

Jensen Huang (Nvidia) says that if he had known the pain, shame, and vulnerability, "nobody in their right mind would do it."

**Lesson:** Building is hard and lonely. The people who last do it because the problem matters, not because it looks glamorous. Expect bad months. Plan for them: keep costs low, keep a small team, and tell your team the truth.

## Slide 10: The reality (two charts)

The slide shows statistics (the actual figures sit in images, I cannot read them from the file). The point of the slide, per the course: most startups fail. Joe says on slide 12: the goal is to put you in the **10% that succeed**.

**Bingo:** Don't be scared of the 90%. Study why they fail (next slides: people, timing, not knowing the customer) and avoid those exact things.

## Slide 11: "Perhaps all is not dismal?"

Facts from the slide (2009 Kaufman Study): about 25,800 active companies founded by MIT alumni, about 3.3 million jobs, about $2 trillion revenue a year, equal to the 11th largest economy in the world.

**Lesson:** Startups, taken together, make jobs and wealth. A fraction of Bingos, done right, matter a lot. For Myanmar: each shop we help grows can hire a packer or rider.

## Slides 24–35: Lessons learned — the 4 critical components

The four components: **Ideas, Execution, Timing, People.** All four are needed.

### Slide 26: What makes an idea valuable?
"Ideas are a dime a dozen." Ask:
- **Value to someone — who?** A named seller type.
- **How much value?** Hours saved per week, orders no longer lost. Measure it.
- **Cost to produce/deliver?** Hosting, support, SMS/notifications (we dropped SMS from one plan).
- **How to capture?** Subscription.
- **Easily copied?** Yes, software is. So our edge = local focus, speed, trust, Burmese-language support, and courier relationships.

### Slide 27: Ideas need Execution
Quote: "Vision without Execution is Hallucination." (The PDF attributes it to Thomas Edison; the spelling "Edision" on the slide is a typo. I did not verify the quote's origin.) The speaker passed on deals like ZipCar and an eBay precursor because the team couldn't execute.

**Bingo:** A beautiful plan with no working product = hallucination. Ship small, working pieces and show them.

### Slide 28: Mens et Manus ("mind and hand")
MIT's motto: ideas + doing. **But** the lesson on the slide: **Ideas + Execution are necessary but NOT sufficient.** You still need timing and people.

### Slide 29: Timing
"I have lost more money and time being ahead of the curve." It can take a long time to be an overnight success: 3D printing about 25 years, Prodigy (about $1B) then Amazon, fusion 50+ years and counting.

**Bingo:** Being too early kills as surely as being too late. Check that sellers want this NOW. If they don't, wait, or narrow the product.

### Slides 30–32: People — the biggest single source of failure
- Slide 31 (Alice and the Cheshire Cat): if you don't know where you're going, any road will do. **Bingo:** write a one-sentence goal (see Vision below) and check every task against it.
- Slide 32: Other people-related failures:
  - **Building the team before understanding the customer/market.** The example: built a football team, then needed basketball players — a painful pivot. **Bingo:** talk to sellers first, hire second.
  - **Not knowing that you don't know.** Example: E-Ink thought it needed $15M for an $80M outcome, but it took $160M. **Bingo:** budget with big safety margins, and list unknowns early.

### Slide 33: Relative importance — Technical vs Business
The slide shows a chart of how technical and business skills compare in importance (the chart is a picture; I can't read the exact values). The takeaway: you need **both**. **Bingo:** we are strong on technical; the gap to fill is business: sales, partnerships, operations, legal.

### Slide 34: Case studies (four companies)

- **VideoCo:** all 4 → acquired in 2 years.
- **HIV-Co:** ideas, execution, timing but weak people → almost failed, modest success.
- **NanoCo:** ideas and execution, but timing and people missing → great potential, failed.

## Slides 36–37: Mission/Vision and the "supported vision"

### Slide 36: Mission / Vision statement (Steve Blank's XYZ)
Formula: **"We help X do Y by doing Z."** X = who, Y = their goal or problem, Z = how.

**Bingo draft:**

### Slide 37: A supported vision (the pitch ladder)

A short thing on top, backed by deeper work underneath:

| Level | Length | Bingo job |
|---|---|---|
| Mission/Vision | 1 sentence | The XYZ above |
| Elevator pitch | 30 seconds | Problem, solution, who, why us |
| Executive summary | 2–4 pages | Write after the survey |

| Full plan | Long | Financials, tech, IP, market, competitors, sales, team |

Supporting foundation: financial projections, technology, intellectual property, market analysis, competitive analysis, sales and distribution, team. "Reality Distortion Field" on the slide = a charismatic founder's pull, but the foundation must be real underneath; do not rely on charm.

**Bingo:** We already have requirements, system design, user stories, a competitor comparison, and a deep plan. Missing: verified market numbers, financial projections, legal review.

## Potholes list for Bingo (my version of "a dozen real challenges")

2. Couriers won't share tracking data. *Plan: manual updates and rider scan first.*
3. Facebook/Messenger changes rules or blocks access.
4. Payment and cash-on-delivery trust.
5. Unstable power and internet. *Our design already uses an offline queue.*
6. Burmese-language quality and Unicode/Zawgyi font issues.
7. Legal entity, foreign ownership, and data-protection rules **[TO CHECK]**.
8. Co-founder or team conflict.
9. Running out of money before sellers pay.
10. A bigger competitor copies the idea.
11. Support load grows faster than the team.
12. Name clash: trademark and domain for "Bingo"/"BingoMart" not checked.
