## The big idea of Part 2, in one breath

**The only thing a business cannot live without is customers.** Great technology is not enough (slide 1). Before building, ask: *Should I do it?* (not *Could I do it?*), *who wants it, can I find them, will they pay?*

Unmet need → better solution → customers → revenue greater than costs → make enough money.
Two questions on the chain: **how much money per customer?** and **how many customers?** And: **how will you get them?**

### Slides 11–12: Different customers for the guitar business
- **Kids (slide 11):** the kid has the need, **the parents pay.** Bob guesses $50/month per kid × 100 kids.
- **Adults 30s–40s and older singles (slide 12):** they pay more ($50/hour, about 8 times a month) and want group jams and a social life.

### Slide 17: How this affects raising money
- Investors' basic fear: **they will lose their money.**
- Their non-negotiable need: **customers.** They ask: Do you have any? Will you? Do you know you need them?

**Bingo:** An investor pitch with zero real sellers will fail. Get even 5–10 real sellers using it, or 20 signed letters of intent, before talking to investors.

## Slides 18–26: Example #1 — Regain (a failure)

**The story (slides 18–22).** Bob ran a nutrition division. Dialysis patients (400,000, fluid-restricted, malnourished) needed supplements, but every supplement was a liquid. His team made a food bar with no fluid, ran a clinical trial, published it in a respected journal, asked doctors and dietitians if they'd recommend it, checked that no competitor had a non-liquid, projected a big market, and launched.

**Slides 23–24: Result: DISASTER.** Sales were under 10% of forecast. Why?
- They asked the **clinicians**, who don't buy it and don't eat it.
- The **patients** didn't want it and couldn't afford it.
- **Retailers** wouldn't carry it (and they were customers too).

**Slide 25: How it hurt him.** A planned stock-market launch was at risk, he had to report bad results to colleagues every month for a whole year ("a slow-motion train wreck").

**Slide 26: What they did.** Learned inside sales, learned local patient publications, got Medicaid reimbursement in 40 states, "felt really bad", and sold the business.

**Lessons for Bingo:**
1. A good product that "works" is not a sold product.
2. **Ask the person who pays and uses it**, not an expert who only recommends it. For BingoMart, don't rely on couriers' or influencers' opinions; ask sellers who would pay.
3. Check every link: patient (user), doctor (influencer), retailer (channel). For BingoMart: seller, buyer, courier.
4. Opinion surveys are weak evidence. Real money or a signed promise is strong evidence.
5. Bad news early is cheaper than bad news after a year.

## Slides 27–42: Example #2 — NiteBite (a success)

**Slides 27–32: the need and the product.** New company with Harvard scientists. 10 million diagnosed diabetics, 4 million on insulin. People on insulin risk low blood sugar at night, and their greatest fear was "dying in my sleep". The answer: a patented timed-release glucose bar, tasty, with a non-medical name that looks like an energy bar, not medicine.

**Slide 33: How they planned.**
1. Involved clinicians, parents and patients in the formula.
2. Interviewed prospects about concerns, price, where they'd buy, whom they trust.
3. Went to support-group meetings.
4. Picked an ad agency that knew diabetes (first ad: flying pigs).

**Slide 34: Results.** The phone volume ramp: 100 calls/day (more phones), 200 (more sales staff), 300 (a database), 500 (rented tables for extra computers). They got into all major pharmacy chains and wholesalers **without paying slotting fees** (fees to get shelf space). Lesson: understanding the customer led to a better product and a successful business.

**Slides 35–36: Who influences the customer.** The best channel was **Certified Diabetes Educators** (15,000 in the USA, 12,000 in the AADE group). Their concern: patient compliance. They hold monthly patient meetings and need new content. NiteBite said "it works and your patients will love it", educators told patients (a free "high quality sales force"), educators told pharmacists, and pharmacists agreed to stock it. **Why no slotting fees?** Because customers were already asking for the product. That demand was their negotiating power.

**Slide 37 (selling flow chart):** Inside sales → educators → patients → retailers. The "new definition of customer" arrow shows that retailers became a customer too.

**Slides 38–39: Retail close.** Callers got qualifying questions, then a free sample, then follow-up (did you get it, what did you think, would you order with free shipping), then re-order calls. Close with the consumer: "Would you like to buy it where you buy your insulin?" Close with the pharmacy: "Your customers want it and you don't have it. What can we do?" Their first order, then bigger re-orders.

**Slides 40–41: Wholesalers and chains.** At a trade show, they called 25 drug stores' wholesaler: "I've already sold them, and built in a profit for you. Put us in your system." Then, for chains: "How would you like us to answer their slotting question?"

**Slide 42: Results.** Fast sales growth, extremely high re-order rate, publicity, several offers to buy, and a sale to a $1 billion pharma company.

**What Bingo copies from NiteBite:**
- Talk to customers in the places they gather (seller groups) and sit in their meetings.
- Find the **trusted influencer**: who do sellers believe? (seller community admins, courier staff, successful shop owners **[TO CHECK] who**.)
- Create demand first, then ask the channel (couriers, payment partners) to join. Demand is our negotiating power.
- Give a free sample (free trial for a first seller), then follow up, then ask for payment, then check re-order/renewal.
- Look at pain: the customer's biggest fear. For sellers it's the buyer saying "where is my parcel?" and trust lost.

### Slide 45: Lessons Bob hopes he learned

| Lesson | Simple meaning | Bingo |
|---|---|---|
| Know your customers intimately: what are they really buying? | Not the feature, the result | Peace of mind and saved time |
| Products "for everyone" aren't for anyone; positioning is imperative ("air for scuba divers") | Pick a narrow group and a sharp message | "BingoMart is for Messenger sellers who ship parcels" |

| Sell the benefit first, then the "reason to believe" | Claim, then proof | "No lost orders" + a tracking page demo |

### Slide 46: Market segmentation by motivation
A ladder from "feeling the pain now" → "worried" → "vigilant" → "forget it". **Start with the people already feeling the pain now.** Ignore people who don't care yet (the "graveyard").

**Bingo:** Start with sellers who just lost an order or got an angry "where is my parcel?" message last month. They are "feeling the pain now". Find them in the survey by asking "when did this last happen?".

### Slide 47: Changing buying habits needs a lot of motivation
Powerful motivators: **pain, fear, greed, vanity.** "Virtue is a tough sell." Questions: what motivator was Regain addressing (weak, mostly "should"), and NiteBite (strong, fear of dying in sleep).

**Bingo's motivators:**
- **Pain:** lost orders, angry buyers. Strongest.
- **Fear:** losing the buyer's trust, losing money to fake or missing orders.
- **Greed:** more sales from looking professional and repeat buyers.
- **Vanity:** a professional tracking link with the shop's name.
Sell pain and fear first.

### Slide 48: The point
Find who finds your offer **both important and unique (or at least better)**, find them and tell them, and take their order.

## Slides 49–54: Power marketing and the sales model

### Slide 49: Power marketing in two easy steps
1. Find out what your customers want.
2. Give it to them.

### Slide 50: "It's not sales, it's forensics"
Instead of promoting a product, **find your customer.** Customers "hire" products to do a job. Design for one specific job and one kind of customer, then go find them; if you did it right, selling is easy.

**Bingo's "job":** "When a buyer asks where their parcel is, help me answer in 5 seconds without searching chats."

### Slide 51: Ways to do that
- **Talk to prospects.** Reports and social media tell you only so much. (He has a "brief rant" about social media for market research: posts are public performance, not real buying behavior.)
- **Know them:** how do they see their problem, what do they buy now, what do they read, whom do they go to, who are their influencers?
- Understand what's behind what they ask for. (The slide references a Henry Ford quote about asking customers; the saying "faster horses" is commonly attributed to him, but I have not verified that he said it.)

**Bingo plan:** 20 real conversations with sellers (call or chat), with the survey as the first net. Do not rely only on Facebook posts or likes.

### Slide 52: You do have competition
- If customers aren't spending money now on the problem, it's **not a problem.**
- Whatever they spend on now **is your competition.** How is yours better in their eyes? Customers buy **value.**

**Bingo competition (what sellers use now):** Facebook/Messenger chat, notebooks, spreadsheets, Viber, calling couriers, courier apps, other local tools (we made a "MyanSan comparison" doc). Real spending on each: **[TO CHECK]**.

### Slide 53: More lessons for the sales model
1. Even if customers need it, if they don't want it, you can't sell it.
2. Early on, it doesn't matter who *needs* it, it matters who *wants* it.
3. If you can't find your customers, you can't sell.
4. If you can't communicate your benefits in seconds, you can't sell.

### Slide 54: The acid test — the 30-second sell + homework
- If you can't explain it to a customer in **30 seconds**, you're in trouble (websites and brochures need focus; the benefits may be unclear).
- If you can't explain it to an investor in 30 seconds, you're unlikely to raise money.
- **Customers buy benefits.** This takes more work than you'd think.
- Homework for next Tuesday: pitch your business in **3 minutes, then in 30 seconds.**

**Bingo 30-second pitch (draft, edit freely):**
> "Do you sell on Facebook and lose track of orders or parcels? BingoMart is a simple shop tool for Myanmar sellers: it keeps every order in one place and gives your buyer a link to see where their parcel is. Fewer lost orders, fewer 'where is my parcel?' messages. Try it free for your next 20 orders."

**3-minute version adds:** the problem story (a seller who lost an order), how it works in 3 steps, who it's for, what it costs **[GUESS]**, and what happens next (free trial, then a plan).

### Break-even arithmetic (item 1 ÷ item 2)

Assumes every paying shop pays monthly and nothing else costs money.

| Plan | Price/month | Paying shops needed for 5,000,000 MMK |
|---|---|---|
| 300,000 MMK plan | 300,000 | 5,000,000 ÷ 300,000 = 16.7, so **17 shops** |
| 500,000 MMK plan | 500,000 | 5,000,000 ÷ 500,000 = **10 shops** |

Against 21,000 registered sellers, 17 shops is about 0.08%. So the market is not the problem; the question is whether a small shop will pay 300,000 MMK a month.

**Warnings (honest):**
- **Price check.** 300,000 MMK is about $143 at the official rate (~2,100 MMK/USD) and about $68 at the real market rate (~4,400 MMK/USD, Dec 2025 report; may be out of date). That is a lot for a Facebook-scale seller. It might suit larger shops. Test two small prices too (for example 30,000 and 100,000 MMK) in the survey before fixing the plan.
- **Free tier costs money.** Every free shop still costs hosting and support. Cap the free tier (for example, a limited number of orders per month).
- **5,000,000 MMK is spending only.** It is the break-even revenue, not profit. Payment fees and taxes **[TO CHECK]** come out of revenue.
- **Exchange rate moves.** Costs you pay in dollars (hosting, tools) change with the kyat. Re-do the arithmetic if the rate shifts.
